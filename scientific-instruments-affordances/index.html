Genealogical working bibliography

Affordances and Scientific Instruments

From ecological perception to designed artefacts, sociomaterial technologies, material culture, and the historical affordances of scientific instruments.

Genealogy at a glance.
Gestalt “demand character” / environmental valence → J. J. Gibson (1966, 1977, 1979): organism–environment possibilities for action.
↳ Design / HCI: Norman (1988) → Gaver (1991) → Norman (1999): perceived, hidden, false, and designed affordances.
↳ Social / technological materiality: Costall (1995) → Hutchby (2001) → Chemero (2003) → Leonardi / Faraj & Azad (2010–14): affordances as relational ways to describe what artefacts enable and constrain in situated practice.
↳ Anthropology / archaeology / material culture: Ingold (1992/2000) → Knappett (2005) → Malafouris (2013) → Rietveld & Kiverstein (2014): skill, embodied action, material engagement, and historically/culturally structured landscapes of possibilities.
↳ History of science / instruments: instrument-materiality work of the 1990s–2000s + Heering’s replication studies → Isis instrument focus (2011) → Hakim’s explicit “historical affordance” (2013) → Jardine on paper affordances (2017) → later material-history and re-enactment work.

Reading key: sections I–IV contain works that explicitly develop or transport the affordance concept. Sections V–VI distinguish the smaller literature that explicitly applies it to historical scientific artefacts from adjacent instrument/practice scholarship that supplies much of the usable historical method without necessarily using the word “affordance.” Last revised 31 August 2026.

VII

How to read the genealogy

Three distinctions are especially useful when applying this bibliography to historical instruments. First, Gibsonian affordances are relational action possibilities, whereas Norman’s design tradition often shifts attention toward what uses an artefact makes perceptible to a user. Second, social and archaeological extensions ask how learned skill, convention, institutions, and historically specific practices shape which affordances become available or salient. Third, instrument historians add temporal instability: an artefact’s possible uses, manipulations, repairs, evidential roles, and meanings can change as it moves between makers, laboratories, collections, demonstrations, and new experimental problems. The most directly relevant methodological bridge for historians of scientific instruments is therefore the line running from Hutchby / material culture through Heering’s practice-oriented replication work to Arnold & Söderqvist, Hakim, Jardine, and related studies of historical objects in use.
